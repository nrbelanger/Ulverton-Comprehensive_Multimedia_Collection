Spinning Mule Maintenance Guide

Lubricants

Lubricant Guide

Lubrication is the most common form of preventative maintenance. Lubricants are used on gears, pins and bushings, chains, pulleys, and rollers. There are many different types of lubricants with different applications and purposes. Early lubricants used on the mill were likely to be seed oils, whale oils and later petroleum derivatives. Grease was also used in situations of higher loads and greater temperatures.

Gears

Gears Guide

Gears are the most common component of the mill's main drive head and are the primary way forces are distributed in the mill. The best practice for preventative maintenance of gears is a methodical visual inspection before attempting to start the mill.

Similar to other mechanical components, all types of gears including spur gears, helical gears, bevel gears, and racks should be well maintained so they can perform efficiently. Here are some things to look out for when conducting maintenance:

Lubrication

Lubrication is necessary for any gear mechanism to function at its best. It enables gears to move smoothly and limits the temperature increase resulting from friction. Lack of lubrication or using the wrong type of lubricant can damage even high quality gears, causing tooth wear and premature failure.

The lubricant choice depends on the gear's rotating speed and tangential speed. Generally, grease lubricants are used for low-speed gears, while splash lubrication and forced oil lubrication benefit medium and high-speed gears. Make sure lubricants are clean and free of contaminants like dust.

Wear and Tear

Due to constant friction, gear teeth may wear out sooner. Check your gears carefully for any signs of wear and tear so you can replace parts as needed. It is also important to use the proper type of lubricant at proper intervals. Specifically, the gear flanks in spur gears are always in contact with the teeth of another gear and will result in normal wear and tear.

Broken or Chipped Gear Teeth

Although certain machines can still function with a chipped or broken gear tooth, it could affect performance and output. Replacing the gear might be the best option. You can avoid broken teeth by making sure the gear has sufficient lubrication and by avoiding high stress or excessive loads.

Knowing the Conditions

Many types of gears can last for decades without ongoing maintenance — everything depends on the nature of the gear and conditions of use. Pay close attention to:

  • Gear materials — some materials are more durable than others. For example, phosphor bronze has high tolerance of wear, while ceramic gears may be more vulnerable to damage over time.
  • Loads — gears that bear heavy loads are more vulnerable to wear, though this can be compensated for with gear design.
  • Speeds — high-speed applications tend to wear gears out faster due to the sheer amount of contact and number of rotations.
  • Temperatures — some materials break down faster at high temperatures.
  • Other harsh conditions — exposure to water or other elements may cause gears to wear down faster.
Ropes

Rope and Pulley Maintenance Guide

Manmade fibers, as well as being much stronger than natural fibers, are very tolerant of those factors that reduce the working life of the latter. Physical damage must be guarded against — rope under tension is easily damaged by chafe. Lines under tension but static (e.g., mooring lines) should be protected at any angular point or rough surface by placing plastic tubing over the rope or wrapping it with canvas.

The effects of chafe from normal friction surfaces such as sheaves, fairleads, and cleats is considerably reduced if the bearing surfaces are large. The groove of a sheave should have a radius that supports one-third of the rope's circumference. Whenever possible, avoid making sharp bends in a rope.

Securing a Coil

When coiling a line, always begin at the end that is made fast so that any twists or kinks can be chased along and run out at the far end. Never apply load to a line when there is a kink in it — doing so will almost inevitably damage and weaken it. Nearly all laid ropes are right-handed and should be coiled clockwise to ensure smooth running out. To ensure even wear, change ropes end-for-end occasionally.

Key Rules for Rope Care

  • Intended use — use rope only for its intended application to add life to the product and reduce the possibility of failure.
  • Avoid kinks — pulling a kink through a restricted space such as a tackle block will seriously damage the rope fibers.
  • Avoid abrasion — where rope must rub over an object, protect it with chafing gear such as canvas wrapped and tied around the rope.
  • Avoid overloading — safe working strength for rope varies from 5:1 to 12:1. If your rope is old or worn, downgrade it to a less critical application or replace it.
  • Avoid sudden strain — rope strong enough under a steady strain can be broken with a sudden jerk. Avoid shock loads whenever possible.
  • Avoid sharp angles — sharp bends greatly affect rope strength. Pad sharp angles for safety.
  • Reverse ends — occasionally reverse your rope end-for-end to distribute wear more evenly.
  • Keep rope clean — dirt embedded in rope acts as an abrasive. Wash with clean water when dirty; air-dry natural fiber ropes before storing.
  • Avoid improper storage — keep synthetic ropes out of sunlight and extremely hot rooms. Natural fiber ropes must be kept dry to prevent rot.

Belt Drive Maintenance

Routine preventive maintenance is essential if a belt drive is to operate correctly. Belt maintenance should include regular condition checks, alignment, and tension checks, as well as frequent inspections of the sheaves and shafts.

When installing V-Belts, never force a belt on with a screwdriver — this can damage the belt. Begin increasing the distance between sheaves by turning the jacking bolts until the belt is snug but not tight, then use a belt tension gauge to tighten to the manufacturer's recommendation. Check tension again after 24 hours of operation.

When replacing a belt, always replace all belts in a multi-belt drive. Belts stretch with use — replacing only one belt means it will be tighter than the others, not meeting the horsepower requirements the drive was designed for.

Chain Drive Maintenance

Chain drives transmit power between a drive unit and a driven unit. An effective preventive maintenance program will provide extended life to a chain drive system.

  • Inspect chain links for wear regularly.
  • Lubricate the chain with lightweight oil recommended by the chain manufacturer.
  • Inspect sprockets for worn teeth and abnormal wear on the sides.
  • Realign sprockets using the same method used for belt drives.
  • Ensure the chain has the proper amount of sag (about ¼" per foot of span).

When replacing any portion of a chain drive, replace all sprockets and chain together. Worn sprockets create changes to the pitch diameter, and worn or "stretched" chains change their pitch diameter beyond limits — both cause premature failure.
